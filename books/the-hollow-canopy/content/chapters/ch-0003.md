The briefing tent had been squared away within an inch of its life, which was Nia's love language and Calder's favorite kind of luck. Map on the board. Radios quiet. The science team's second bird still an hour out, so the room held only the detail, the linguist who had come in on the first lift, and the man Calder was already failing to predict.

Dr. Maren stood at the map as if he had been invited to grade it. He had declined coffee. He had accepted nothing else, including the chair. The scar through his eyebrow caught the lamp when he tilted his head, which he did whenever someone else was talking — not impatient, measuring, as if speech were a specimen that might bolt if he moved too fast. His scent was a locked door. Blockers, yes, but under them an absence so complete Calder's hindbrain kept checking the locks. Rain. Antiseptic. Nothing that asked to be known.

It bothered him. He filed the bother under professional instinct and did not examine the filing.

"Helix will not hike the tourist route," Calder said, tracing the river with a knuckle. "If they're in Virelia they're already off-map, which means they bought a guide or they bought a satellite that doesn't officially exist. We go upriver at first light. Two boats. You ride mine. You do not peel off to look at a flower unless the flower has filed a request."

"Flowers rarely do," Dr. Maren said.

"That's the spirit."

The linguist — Dr. Raman, Priya, who had shaken his hand like a woman completing a form — glanced at Maren. Something moved across her face, quick as a fish. Maren did not look at her. The something died. Calder clocked it and let it sit. People had histories. His job was to notice which histories grew teeth.

"Your packet underestimates the Reach," Maren said.

Nia, at the side of the board, shifted her weight. She liked it when civilians were wrong in manageable ways. She liked it less when their tone suggested the manageable part was his problem.

"Enlighten me," Calder said. He kept it pleasant. Pleasant was a tool. "I hate being underestimated almost as much as I hate being eaten."

Maren stepped to the map. He did not ask permission, which Calder should have minded and didn't, quite. The doctor's finger found the red circle and then moved past it, into the blank, with the certainty of a man who had already been there in every way except his feet.

"This treats the interior as empty with hazards," he said. "It isn't empty. Survey Lantern's last reliable transmission described biological repetition. Growth rings at intervals that aren't annual. Insects vectoring mineral particulates. Radio behavior that mimics distress without originating as distress. If your threat model is mercenaries plus jaguars, you will misread the first thing that kills us, and you will misread it as an animal or a person. It may be neither."

The tent listened. Harun had stopped pretending to clean a belt. Jun's eyes were half-lidded and entirely present. Rhee leaned forward as if the map might start transmitting out of spite.

Calder felt the snag again, harder. The cadence. The pause before the important clause. The way Maren waited to see whether the room was worth the rest of the sentence. He had heard that pause. He could not find the room it belonged to. The failure itched like a half-remembered song, and he had the absurd urge to laugh at himself for it.

"You're saying the jungle is the opposing force," he said.

"I'm saying the jungle is a system that has already eaten one expedition and filed the paperwork as a river." Maren's voice stayed level. He could have been discussing a budget. "Helix is real. I read the same brief you did. They want what Lantern brushed and did not survive. If we treat every anomaly as cover for a human enemy, we will walk into the anomaly while aiming at the wrong tree line. I won't do that to keep your model tidy."

"And I won't let you walk into it to keep your paper tidy." Calder heard the edge and sanded it, a little, because Nia was watching his sanding with professional interest. "You're the authority on the system. I'm the authority on whether you are standing in it unprotected. Those are compatible if you don't treat a perimeter as a suggestion."

"Perimeters are suggestions whenever the data is on the other side of them."

"That sentence is going to age badly."

"Most honest sentences do."

There it was: a flicker of dry humor, gone before Calder could decide to enjoy it. He did enjoy it. That was annoying. He had wanted an arrogant eccentric he could manage with jokes and a firm hand on a pack strap. He had been given a man who looked at a blank map and saw a mechanism, who did not raise his voice, who stood inside an alpha's attention as if attention were weather he had dressed for.

Calder had met omegas who performed calm and omegas who didn't have to. This was the second kind, and it made the first kind look like a costume. It also made Calder aware, sharply, of his own height in the tent, of the habit he had of taking up a door. He stepped back half a pace without making a speech about it. Maren's eyes noted the step. Of course they did.

"Medical," Calder said, because he needed a protocol more than he needed to win the map. "Mateo."

Mateo did not soften it. Calder had hired him, in the ways you could hire a man already assigned to you, for exactly that. "Suppressants are logged. I'll draw a confirmation sample before we embark, with Dr. Abebe countersigning when his bird lands. If your levels slip, or if something in the field mimics a slip, alphas clear the cordon. Betas hold watch. That includes the major. It is not a commentary on your judgment. It is how we keep biology from writing checks the mission can't cash."

Maren nodded once. "Acceptable. I won't perform distress to test you, and I won't hide it if the compound fails. I'd like the same honesty if one of your alphas starts a rut and decides heroism is a substitute for a patch."

Harun coughed a laugh into his fist. Jun's mouth did a small, treacherous thing that was almost a smile. Calder felt his own ears go warm, which was ridiculous, which was fine, which he would not be examining.

"You'll have it," he said. "I'm many things. A cliché is not one of them."

"Everyone is a cliché to someone," Maren said. "The work is noticing which one you've agreed to."

The pause again. The head tilt. Calder's memory reached, closed on nothing, and came back with a ridiculous image: pencil shavings on a school desk, a window, rain not unlike this rain. He had been seventeen and unbearable. The image had no face in it. He let it go before it could make him look as unsettled as he was.

Priya Raman was watching him as if he were a line she had already translated and did not like.

"Dr. Raman," he said, "anything to add, or are you saving it for when I'm not in the room?"

"I save my best work for stone," she said, mild. "And for people who ask the right question. You haven't yet."

"Give me a year."

"You have less."

Maren turned from the map. Up close the calm was even more impressive, because Calder could see what it cost in the tiny muscles at the jaw, held and held and held. A man who did not want to be read. Calder respected that. He also, inconveniently, wanted to read him anyway. Professional curiosity. He insisted on the adjective.

"Then here's the shape of it," Calder said. "You are ORCHID. I am the wall. My team follows me because they like living, not because I shout. You will eat when Mateo says your hands are doing the thing hands do when chemists forget food. You will sleep. You will tell me before you do something I would call brave and you would call obvious. In exchange I will listen when you say the threat is not the thing with the gun. I am capable of learning. It offends my reputation, so I'd appreciate discretion."

For a moment he thought Maren might smile. The moment passed through the room and did not land.

"Major," Maren said, "if you are the wall, try to keep up when the wall is in the wrong place."

"Was that a yes?"

"It was a description of the work."

Nia rubbed her forehead in the manner of a woman adding a new category to a spreadsheet titled *Reasons the Major Will Be Insufferable*. Calder felt the old, reliable lift in his chest — the one that came before a hard job, when the jokes got better because the stakes had introduced themselves. He looked at Maren and the lift tangled with that other thing, the snag, the pencil shavings, the sense that he had just been evaluated by a judge who had seen an earlier draft of him and had not kept the draft for sentimental reasons.

Impossible. He had never met this man. He would remember a mouth like that. He would remember eyes that refused to flinch.

"Boats at 0500," he said. "Rhee, I want the ghost on a speaker in my boat, volume low. If it changes its sentence, I hear it before the trees do. Dr. Maren, you're with me. Dr. Raman, second boat with Nia, unless you'd rather supervise my education personally."

"I would rather survive it," Priya said.

Maren picked up his case. At the tent flap he paused, and the pause was so familiar Calder almost said *wait* as if they were mid-conversation in a life he hadn't lived. Rain darkened the doctor's shoulders. He looked back once. Not quite at Calder's eyes. At the scar, or at the rank, or at a point in the air where a younger man might have stood if the years had been kinder or crueler.

"Major," he said. "The thing on the radio. Don't answer it. And don't ask it what it means until I have a baseline. Some systems treat a question as consent."

Then he was gone into the weather, Priya a half-step behind, and the tent exhaled.

Harun whistled, soft. "He's going to be a problem."

"He's going to be alive," Calder said.

Jun reassembled a silence and then broke it. "You like him."

"I like competence. Try not to spread rumors. I'm fragile."

Nia waited until the others found tasks. She stood at his shoulder and spoke to the map, which was how she delivered the sentences he could not dodge.

"You went somewhere," she said. "In the middle. Your face did the thing."

"My face is a closed file."

"Your face is a bar fight with subtitles." She tapped the red circle. "If you know him, I need to know before the river. Conflicts of history get people killed more often than jaguars."

Calder looked at the flap, at the rain that had taken the scientist, at his own hand still shaped around a pointer he had forgotten to put down.

"I don't know him," he said. It was true. It felt like a lie he would be asked to repeat. "I don't know him, Nia. That's what worries me."

Outside, the second helicopter was still a rumor. The ghost on Rhee's recorder kept its nineteen-minute faith. And somewhere in the camp a man who smelled like nothing walked through weather he had already decided to question, carrying a name he had not given, while Calder Rhys — confident, decorated, and suddenly imprecise — stood over a map of a place that did not intend to be known and tried to remember a window.
