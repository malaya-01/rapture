Nia Okonkwo counted people the way other officers counted rounds: not because she distrusted them, but because a missing number was how the last asset had become a story instead of a person.

Dawn at the jetty was a rumor. The river came out of the canopy the color of tea left too long, and the rain had stopped performing and started residing. Two boats knocked their hulls against the tires nailed along the dock. Base Lantern's lights were a sullen yellow behind them. Ahead, the map ended.

She stood with the manifest clipboard under her arm and watched the second helicopter's passengers become a camp.

Dr. Anouk Solano hit the dock like a woman who had already argued with the river and won a postponement. Omega, archaeologist, bonded, and entirely uninterested in being managed by anyone who had not read the site report. Mateo met her halfway. They did not kiss. They put their foreheads together for one breath, his hand at the back of her neck, hers fisted in his sleeve, and then Anouk said, "If you packed the resin kit under the morphine again I will divorce you in front of the eels."

"The morphine is labeled," Mateo said. "Your resin is labeled. The jungle is not a filing cabinet."

"Then stop using my filing cabinet as a seat."

He laughed, short, and she smiled with her eyes only. Nia looked away, not from discomfort but from respect. That was a marriage that already knew how to work. She had seen bonds that were performances and bonds that were accidents. This one was a practice. It made her job easier and harder: easier because they would not destabilize a perimeter for a touch, harder because if the river took one of them the other would keep functioning and she would have to watch the functioning and call it courage.

"Captain." A man in Virelian field greens offered a nod that was almost a salute and declined, wisely, to become one. "Tomas Ibarra. My people hold the dock until you are around the bend. After that, the Reach is yours and God's, and God has been noncommittal."

"We'll take the Reach," Nia said. "Leave God the paperwork."

Ibarra's gaze moved, polite and not empty, to where Dr. Maren was checking vial seals with his back to the boats. "Your scientist doesn't look like a man who needs a wall."

"That's why he has one."

She did not elaborate. Ibarra was decent. Decent men still repeated what they saw, and what she saw was Calder crossing the dock toward Maren with that loose, dangerous cheer he used when he had decided a civilian was going to live even if the civilian objected.

"Doctor," Calder said. "Boat one. You're with me, Harun, Jun, and Rhee. If you fall in, Harun will be loud about it and Jun will shoot whatever is encouraged by the splash. Try not to encourage the splash."

Maren did not look up from the seal. "If I fall in, Major, it will be because the data is wetter than the boat. I'll try to schedule it."

"Was that humor?"

"It was a schedule."

Calder grinned as if he had been handed a gift. Maren did not grin back. Nia filed the asymmetry. Calder liked people who could take a joke. Maren was not refusing the joke. He was refusing the warmth under it, with a precision that looked like manners if you had not spent a decade watching people survive men who smiled. She did not know the history. She knew the temperature. She would watch it.

A clatter from the specimen crates cut the temperature in half.

Lieutenant Sable Venn came up the bank mud to the knee, reconnaissance finished, patience not. She stopped in front of a beta in a rain cape who was kneeling over an open case as if the case were a chapel.

"Close it," Sable said.

"It's ventilated," the man said, cheerful, unbothered, which was either bravery or a diagnosis. Dr. Ellis Ward. Field biologist. Nia had his file: published on mineral-vector insects, no combat, a note from a previous team lead that said *walks toward things*. "If I close it fully she'll overheat. She's a she, I think. The wing dust is—"

"I don't care what the wing dust is. You opened a live catch inside my return lane."

"Your return lane was between me and the tree she was already leaving." Ellis lifted the case a careful inch. Inside, something pale beat once, a moth the size of a palm, wings dusted as if it had flown through a quarry. "She isn't a weapon. She's a map that hasn't agreed to be paper yet."

Sable stared at him. Alpha, blocker-flat, still somehow occupying more air than the explanation deserved. "If it bites anyone, I will put it in the river and you can map that."

"If it bites anyone I'll apologize to the moth," Ellis said, and shut the case to a gap, not a seal. "She was here before your lane."

Nia stepped in before Sable spent a career on a moth. "Case stays closed past a finger-width. It rides boat two, lashed, not in anyone's lap. Lieutenant, you're on boat two with me. Dr. Ward, if your map escapes, you retrieve it. You do not send my recon officer into the trees after a feeling."

Ellis blinked, then nodded as if he had been given a reasonable law by a reasonable country. "Yes, Captain."

Sable looked at Nia like a woman who had been denied a fight and would remember the debt kindly. "Prints on the north bank," she said, switching to work because work was how she put her knife away. "Boot-adjacent. Not ours. Not fresh enough to be Helix this morning. Something heavy walked where a person would, and then didn't."

"Show me on the water," Nia said. "Not here."

Professor Hester Lang arrived last, because Hester Lang arrived when she had finished disapproving of the journey. Fifty-eight, alpha, a blocker patch worn like an insult, a canvas satchel older than several of the soldiers. She looked at the tents, the guns, the river, and sniffed.

"You've pitched a war on a survey," she said. "Pavel would have hated the lights."

Anouk, passing with a crate of brushes, did not slow. "Pavel isn't here to hate them. The lights keep us from becoming a footnote he would have also hated."

Hester's mouth tightened. Grief, Nia thought, wearing scholarship as a coat. She let it pass. Grief that still walked toward the boats was grief she could use. Grief that sat down became a rescue.

Rhee found her at the radio crate while the others loaded. The specialist's hood was down. Her eyes were the particular bright of someone who had not slept and did not intend to apologize for the data.

"Four more cycles," Rhee said, and turned the recorder so Nia could see the log. "Same phrase. Same wear on the vowel. Interval held at nineteen minutes within two seconds. Signal strength jumped when the storm thinned and the mast was on the inland bearing. It isn't bouncing off our nets. It isn't in our encryption. If it were a stuck beacon from a dead survey, the power source should have coughed by now. It hasn't coughed."

Nia listened to one cycle. The man's voice came up like something remembered by the water.

*Don't ask it.*

She believed it more than Calder did. Calder believed in threats he could put a team between. Nia had buried a civilian who had been standing exactly where the plan said safety was. She believed in instructions that arrived without a sender, because the last time she had waited for a sender the sender had been the problem.

"You tell the major it's weather again and I'll reassign your vowels," she said.

Rhee almost smiled. "Dr. Maren threatened Dr. Raman with that. It isn't how linguistics works."

"It's how I work." Nia closed the log. "Speaker on his boat, volume low, as he ordered. If the sentence changes, you break into my channel before you finish being clever. We are not doing a six-hour private mystery. Not on this river."

"Yes, Captain."

The boats filled. Nia did the count again, aloud this time, because aloud made people hear themselves as numbers that were expected to come back.

"Boat one: Rhys, Maren, Dalca, Park, Amari. Boat two: me, Venn, Solano, Solano — don't be cute, Mateo, I know which is which — Raman, Ward, Abebe, Lang. Ibarra's section holds the dock. If we are not on this channel by nightfall, you do not come in after us with a hero's idea. You hold, and you call the Compact. Clear?"

"Clear," Ibarra said.

Calder caught her eye from the other hull. The cheer was still there. Under it, the thing she had seen in the briefing tent: a man reaching for a memory and closing his hand on rain. She would kick him if he managed Maren like a problem. She had promised. She intended to keep promises that were still shaped like people.

"Cast off," she said.

The ropes went. The motors took the river. Rain resumed its occupation of the air, not as weather anymore, as the climate of the work. Behind them the dock lights blurred. Ahead, the canopy closed over the water like a mouth that had been patient for a long time, and Nia Okonkwo put her hand on the gunwale and counted, once more, the living she meant to bring back.
