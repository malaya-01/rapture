Sable found the camp because the bank had been trying not to be a camp, and she had a professional dislike of things that tried.

Day three, late light, the river narrowed between stone that looked poured. Nia cut the motor on her signal. The smell hit before the shapes did: old canvas, wet ash, and the sweet-rot of a kitchen that had been abandoned in the middle of believing it would be used again. Platforms clung to the bank on poles. One had slumped into the water. A radio mast lay in the mud like a felled saint, cable still reaching toward a set that was no longer there.

Survey Lantern. She had read the file on the flight in, which was to say she had read a paragraph. Lost to rapids. No bodies recovered. Condolences in a font that had never gotten its boots wet.

"Perimeter," she told the boat, and was already over the side, water to her thighs, rifle up, because perimeters were what you owed people who might still be stupid enough to be alive.

Nia came after, quieter. Calder held boat one on the current with Maren visibly straining toward the bank like a compass that had been told to sit. Good. Let the compass sit. Sable did not want a scientist in a site until she knew whether the site wanted them back.

Ellis Ward slipped in the mud behind her and made a small apologetic sound, as if the mud had feelings.

"I said perimeter," Sable said.

"I'm inside it if you're the edge," he said. "There's scat by the cold fire. I need to see it before your boots make it a philosophy."

She should have sent him back. The last time she had trusted an analyst's note, the note had said *low risk* and the nest had said otherwise, and she had carried a private's name in her mouth for a year. Scientists walked toward things. Things appreciated the courtesy.

Ellis was already crouched, happy, disgusting, careful. "Herbivore. Large. Not a cat. The hair in it is coarse, and there's stone grit, the same grit as the moth dust if the moth dust has cousins. They bedded here after the people left. Or while the people were leaving. The fire was drowned, not burned out. See the ash line? That's a spill, not a rain. Someone upended a pot on purpose."

Sable hated that she needed the sentence. She hated more that it was usable. "How long."

"Not eleven years of occupancy. The platforms are that old. This scat is weeks. Something still comes to the kitchen."

"Wonderful."

She cleared the standing shelter with Jun on the other bank covering the slit of the door. Inside: two cots, a mold-map of a vanished photograph, and a tin case wrapped in oilcloth so lovingly that the love itself was a kind of fear. Sable brought it out and did not open it. Hester Lang came up the bank as if the mud had personally offended her rank as a scholar, saw the case, and held out her hands.

"If that is Pavel's, you will not pry it with a knife," Hester said.

"I wasn't going to pry it with a knife."

"You were thinking about it. Your face is a tactical plan."

Nia nodded. Sable gave the case over. Hester's fingers knew the knot. The oilcloth gave. Inside, a journal swollen at the edges and dry at the heart, which meant someone had sealed it after the river had already had a vote.

Hester opened to the last intact page. Her mouth moved without sound. Then she read, and the reading was worse than a shout because it did not perform.

"The river is repeating us," she said. "Not our words. Our shapes. I watched a heron stand in the eddy for an hour and then stand again, the same hour, the same turn of the head, as if the water had kept the bird and was practicing. Marta says I have river fever. Marta is probably right. If she is not right, do not ask it what it wants. Asking is how you give it a pattern. If you are reading this, you are already late. Don't—"

The page ended where the next had welded itself into a brick of pulp. Hester did not force it. She stood with the journal open and the rain tapping the oilcloth and all the magnificence gone out of her shoulders.

Anouk, who had been photographing a carved post half-sunk by the fire, lowered the camera. "Hester."

"It's his hand," Hester said. "He wrote the way he spoke when he was afraid. Short. He hated short. The file said rapids." She laughed once, an ugly sound, and killed it. "Rapids don't repeat a heron. Rapids don't tell you not to ask."

The quiet that followed scared Anouk; Sable saw it in the way the archaeologist stepped closer, not to comfort, to be a body in the blast radius of a grief that had stopped performing. Sable understood that kind of quiet. It was the quiet after a wrong file. She had worn it.

"Major," Nia said into the radio. "Lantern is here. The obituary is a lie. We're pulling the journal and a pole with tool marks. No bodies. Fresh animal sign. Ward says weeks, not years."

Calder's answer came back thin. "Copy. Maren wants the pole. He can want it from the boat until you say the bank is done."

"Bank isn't done," Sable said, mostly to herself.

She walked the north edge because the north edge was where her earlier prints had wanted to go. Mud, leaf, the ordinary manuscript of a forest. Then a mark that made her stop so cleanly Ellis, trailing, almost walked into her pack.

It was a hoof and it was not. Two slots, deep, and ahead of them a scuff like a boot-heel, and ahead of that the slots again, as if something had remembered halfway through a step what feet were supposed to be. The stride was a person's. The weight was more than a person's. Where the print flexed, the mud had a smear of the same pale grit as the moth wing.

"Don't step in it," Ellis said, softly, which was the first time he had sounded like he was in a place that could kill him.

"I wasn't going to step in it."

"You leaned."

She turned. He was looking at the print with his whole face, delighted and afraid, the two emotions sharing a room without a fight. Rain on his glasses. A smudge of ash on his wrist. Not a soldier. Not useless. The combination offended her training and relieved her anyway.

"What walks like that," she said.

"Something that has seen us," Ellis said. "Or something that used to be nearer to us than a deer gets. I don't have a name. If I invent one to soothe you, it will be a lie, and you seem like a person who keeps lies in a separate box from the dead."

Sable looked back at the camp, at Hester still holding the journal as if it might apologize, at Anouk's hand hovering and not quite landing, at the mast in the mud. The file had said rapids. The ground said a shape practicing how to be a person.

"Stay on my left," she said. "If it has seen us, it can see you better when you wander. I hate needing you. I'm saying it so it doesn't become a surprise later."

Ellis nodded, solemn, and then ruined it by being kind. "I'll try to be needable in a straight line."

She almost smiled. She spent the almost on the treeline instead, rifle up, and led him back toward the boats along a path that did not include the print. Behind them the river kept the camp. Ahead, the radio in Rhee's hands would speak again in a few minutes, a man's voice worn at the vowel, telling them not to ask.

Sable Venn, who had stopped trusting notes, believed a dead page more than she had believed a living analyst in years. She hated the belief. She kept it. On the water, Dr. Maren was already reaching for the pole they had bagged, and Major Rhys was already between the reach and the man, and the jungle folded the bank shut behind them like a file returned to the wrong drawer.
