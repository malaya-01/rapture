Mateo liked wounds better than mysteries. Wounds had edges. You could irrigate an edge. Mysteries got into the suppressant log and started inventing symptoms.

The night camp was a hollow of roots above the shelf, lamps low, rain reduced to a conversation the leaves were having without them. Jun sat on a crate and tolerated antiseptic with the expression of a woman allowing a friend a small idiocy. The graze was exactly what she had claimed: rude, not structural. He closed it with two strips and a lecture she did not need.

"You twitch when you fire now, I'll know," he said.

"I twitch, Harun will hear it from the next ridge and make it a song," Jun said. "The incentive structure is already cruel."

Harun, close enough to be a wall and polite enough not to hover where Nia could cite him, snorted. "I have a beautiful voice."

"You have a voice," Jun said, and let her shoulder touch his knee. A bond, registered, old enough to be bored of its own drama. Mateo approved. Boredom was a clinical sign of health.

Ellis was next. The ankle was a twist, not a break. Ellis narrated the swelling as if it were a colleague. "Heat's local. I'm not going to pretend I can sprint. If something that walks like a person comes back, I can still be needable in a straight line. Sable requested the straight line. I'm treating it as orders."

"You're treating it as a crush," Mateo said, wrapping. "That's allowed. It's not allowed to outrank the wrap. Keep it elevated or I'll have Venn sit on you, and she will enjoy the legal basis."

Ellis went pink, which was informative, and shut up, which was a gift.

Jonah Abebe took the log book out of Mateo's reach without asking, because Jonah had never met a log he trusted in someone else's handwriting. Beta, forty, the moral spine of the drug cabinet. He frowned at the columns.

"Two alphas spiked harder than a graze explains," Jonah said. "Harun and the major. Not rut. Not a broken blocker, I checked the patches. Pupil and pulse say they caught a scent that wasn't ours and wasn't the jungle's ordinary rot. The kites. Membrane residue on Jun's sleeve made my eyes water and I'm not even in the chorus."

"You want to call it a pheromone mimic."

"I want to not call it anything until I have a swab that survives the rain. I'm raising the watch standard. If anyone's suppressant line wobbles tonight — Maren's, Park's — alphas off the cordon. You included, if you're the one who wants to be noble about your wife."

"My wife would sedate me for nobility," Mateo said.

"Good. She's the sensible Solano."

Across the lamp, the sensible Solano was losing a fight she enjoyed. Anouk had a rubbing on a board, taken from the drowned pole: a knot of lines that might have been script and might have been a craftsman's habit. Hester leaned over it with the contempt of a woman who had spent thirty years being right in rooms that applauded the wrong person.

"It's a basin mark," Hester said. "Ritual. You can see the pour-line in the lower groove. They were offering."

"It's a measure," Anouk said. "The pour-line is a meniscus. You don't offer with a meniscus. You measure. If you call every groove a god, you'll walk past the laboratory and name it a church."

"If you call every church a laboratory, you'll bottle the god and act surprised when it drinks you."

"That isn't an argument. That's a mood."

"Moods have kept more scholars alive than your resin."

They would be at it for an hour. Mateo felt a fondness so ordinary it hurt. This was the work: people who loved the same dead city in incompatible dialects, still passing the lamp so the other could see. He caught Anouk's eye. She lifted a brow. Later. He lifted his chin. Alive. The whole marriage, in two gestures, and then she went back to destroying Hester's god with a piece of charcoal.

He found Calder at the edge of the hollow, watching the dark as if the dark had a promotion board.

"Arm," Mateo said.

"It's Jun's."

"It's also yours. You hit the wall with the scientist. Don't make me diagnose you by the way you're standing like a man who won."

Calder gave him the forearm. A bruise, already theatrical. Mateo cleaned nothing, because there was nothing to clean, and stayed because the major's silence had a texture he didn't like.

"He's always like that?" Calder said.

"Maren?"

"ORCHID. The quiet. The — he watched a thing try to open him and he took notes with his eyes. I can't tell if he's brave or if he left his fear in a different jacket."

Mateo thought of the hands he had not been asked to treat. He had seen them anyway, after the tape, from the other boat: a fine tremor, hidden against a notebook, gone by the time anyone with rank looked. Priya had seen it. Priya saw everything and spent it like a miser. He was not going to hand Calder a confidence the doctor hadn't offered. He was going to hand him the clinical version, because clinical versions kept people from being stupid in the dark.

"He's scared," Mateo said. "He does it quietly. You didn't hear that from me. If you walk over there and tell him you've diagnosed his feelings, I will put you on saline and a lecture."

Calder huffed. It wasn't quite a laugh. "I dragged him."

"You dragged him and then you did what he said. He noticed both. That's the whole report."

"He pauses before he talks," Calder said, and then looked annoyed at himself for saying it. "It's nothing. It's a habit. I feel like I've stood next to it before. Nia already asked if I know him. I don't."

"Then stop auditioning for a past," Mateo said. "Sleep. If the kites were shedding some kind of scent-noise, your nervous system is going to write you a very creative dream. I don't want the creative dream on watch."

He left the major with the dark and went to the tent he was allowed to share because the bond was registered and Nia was not a sadist. Anouk came in ten minutes later, charcoal on her thumb, and put her forehead to his without speaking. The rain on the canvas was a kind of permission.

"If it happens," she said, "and it's me or someone bleeding out, you already know."

"I know."

"Say it anyway. I don't want it to be a discovery."

"I stay with the bleed," Mateo said. "You stay alive without me hovering. You don't forgive me for choosing you over a kid with an artery."

"There is no kid."

"There's always a kid. Sometimes the kid is Harun. Sometimes it's a scientist who takes notes while he drowns." He kissed her brow, brief, nothing the rain hadn't already seen. "I'm not going to make you my exception. That's the only way I know how to be married to you out here."

Anouk's hand closed in his shirt. "Good. Because if you hover, I'll assign you to Hester's church, and you'll deserve it."

He smiled into her hair. Outside, the nineteen-minute voice spoke once, indifferent, and the camp held. Mateo Solano closed his eyes with the wrap-count done and the truth delivered and the particular peace of a man who had said the hard thing before the jungle could say it for him.
