By the second day the river had become a corridor with opinions.

Ivo sat on the forward crate of boat one with his notebook in a dry bag and his knees drawn up, because Calder had, without asking, put him where a rifle could see both banks and a hand could reach his collar. The hand had not reached. The intention lived in the air between them anyway, a professional weather. Harun sang badly until Jun told him the fish would file a complaint. Rhee watched the recorder the way other people watched saints. Every nineteen minutes the dead voice arrived, faithful as a metronome.

*Don't ask it.*

Ivo did not ask it anything. He wrote the interval in the margin and the bearing Rhee muttered, and he watched the banks.

The trees were wrong in a way that would have sounded romantic if he had been a worse scientist. Trunks the color of wet iron. Buttress roots like the ribs of something that had lain down on purpose. And the moths.

They were not Ellis's captive, though Ellis would have wept with joy and then tried to give a lecture from the other boat. These moved in a loose ribbon above the water, palm-sized, pale, their wings catching light as if the light had grit in it. When one dipped close to the bow, Ivo saw the veins. Not pigment. Particulate. Stone dust seated in the membrane, arranged, not random — a knot, repeated, too small to read and too deliberate to be accident.

"Don't touch it," Calder said, behind him.

"I wasn't going to touch it."

"You leaned."

"Leaning is how eyes work at distance."

"Leaning is how people become a story I have to tell your institute." Calder's voice was easy. The ease was a skill. Ivo had known a boy who used ease as a door he could shut. The man used it as a handrail. The difference mattered, and Ivo refused to be grateful for it on a boat. "If you need a sample, you say so, and Jun watches the canopy while you take it. You don't collect with your face."

"The wing isn't a sample I can take without killing the map," Ivo said. "The dust is the data. Scraping it is how you get a dead insect and a lie."

Calder was quiet for a beat. The beat was familiar. Ivo hated that he knew the length of it: the half-second in which Calder decided whether to be decent.

"Then we look," Calder said. "We don't grab. You can put that in your schedule."

Ivo did not thank him. Thanking him would have been a door.

They made the sandbar at midday because Mateo, from the following boat, had shouted something about calories and mutiny. The bar was a pale tongue of gravel under trees that dripped even when the rain paused. Nia had tape up before the engines finished coughing — a red line, shin-high, from root to root, the universal religion of people who intended to leave.

Ivo ate half a ration because Priya looked at him from boat two until he did. The bar was loud with small life. Beetles like dropped nails. A frog the color of a bruise. And the moths, the ribbon of them, leaving the river and going inland through a gap in the roots, directly across Nia's tape, as if tape were a hypothesis they had disproved.

He watched the line of them. Three, then seven, then a steady thread. They did not wander. They held a bearing.

The gap on the far side of the tape was a darker green, and in that green a trunk had been cut by time into a cross-section at the height of his chest, a fall so old the moss had made a liturgy of it. The rings were visible. He counted, because counting was how he stayed inside himself. The intervals were not annual. They came in clusters: three close, a wide pale band, three close, a wide pale band, as if the tree had been keeping a calendar of something that happened in bursts.

The answer was on the other side of the tape.

He knew the rule. He also knew that rules were written by people who were not looking at a tree that had tried to write a sentence. He stepped over the line. The tape brushed his shin. A moth passed his ear with a sound like a dry page.

"Maren."

Calder's voice had lost the handrail.

Ivo stopped. He did not turn yet. The rings were a hand's breadth from his notebook. If he turned first he would lose the count. He lifted the notebook, sketched the cluster, and then he turned.

Calder stood on the lawful side of the tape with rain in his hair and no joke anywhere on his face. The change was total and, Ivo admitted in a locked room of his mind, competent. When the man decided someone was in danger, the charm left as if it had been given a lawful order.

"You were told to remain behind the tape," Calder said.

"Yes."

"You crossed it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"The answer was on the other side."

"That is not an acceptable reason."

"It was scientifically acceptable."

Something moved in Calder's jaw. Not the old boredom. Anger, clean, aimed at the risk and not at the correction of a classroom. Ivo felt the difference and resented the feeling for being accurate.

"You don't get to redefine acceptable because a moth did it first," Calder said. "Get back on this side. Now. You can look at the tree from a distance Jun can cover, or you can explain to Nia why I dragged you, and I will drag you."

"The rings aren't readable from the lawful side. The moss—"

"Mar—" Calder stopped.

What came out was a hitch, a swallowed syllable, a man reaching and finding he had grabbed the wrong word. He recovered on a breath. "Doctor. This side. I'm not asking the tree."

Ivo came back over the tape because the count was already in the notebook and because being dragged would have made a scene Priya would interpret. Calder did not touch him. The absence of the touch was its own kind of care, and Ivo set it down very carefully where it could not grow.

They stood a foot apart in the wet gravel. Calder looked at the sketch, upside down, and was silent in the way of a person actually seeing.

"Clusters," he said.

"Not years," Ivo said. "Intervals. Something happened to this tree on a schedule. Or the tree decided a schedule was the same thing as a year. I won't know which until I see another."

"You'll see another from the boat."

"If the boat cooperates."

"Eat the rest of that bar."

"I ate."

"You ate like a man paying a tax." Calder held out the other half, which he had, absurdly, been carrying. "Asset management. Don't make it a moment."

Ivo took it. Their fingers did not meet. He ate because his hands had begun, now that the tape was behind him, to do the small treacherous tremor they saved for after. He turned his body so Calder would see the notebook and not the hands.

Priya, on the other boat, saw the hands. She lifted her chin a fraction. *I see you. I will not say the name.* Ivo hated the kindness and used it.

When he spoke again he waited the old beat, the one he had learned in rooms where speaking too fast got you laughed at. He hated that the beat was still in him. He hated more that Calder's face, listening, went briefly distant, as if the beat had a hallway attached.

"The moths are flying a bearing," Ivo said. "Not feeding. Not mating. If your ghost has a direction, they may share it. I want that noted before someone decides they're pretty."

"Noted," Calder said. His voice had come back to easy, but the easy sat wrong, like a coat put on in a hurry. "Next time the answer is on the other side of a line, you tell me before you become the answer. Clear?"

"Clear," Ivo said.

It was not a surrender. It was a description of the work. He looked at the rings he had not been allowed to touch and felt the Reach look back, patient, mineral, interested. Behind him Calder Rhys stood on the correct side of a piece of tape and did not know the boy he had almost named, and the river kept the nineteen-minute faith of a dead man's warning, and Ivo's hands, hidden against the notebook, took their time remembering how to be still.
