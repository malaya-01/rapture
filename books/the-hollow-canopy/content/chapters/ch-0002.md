Ivo Maren had known for six hours, which was long enough to build a laboratory inside his own pulse and still not long enough to trust the results.

The roster had come up on the cabin screen between a weather overlay and a polite reminder about scent blockers. AEGIS DETACHMENT SEVEN. COMMANDER: MAJ. C. RHYS. The initial had been a courtesy. The world was full of men who could be reduced to C. He had clicked through because he was precise, and precision was how he failed to protect himself.

Calder Rhys. Thirty-two. Alpha. The photograph was official and therefore a little dishonest: dress uniform, the scar on the jaw newer than any memory Ivo had a right to, the mouth arranged in the expression of a person who expected to be liked. Fourteen years had done what fourteen years do. They had kept the eyes. They had made a weapon out of a boy and then congratulated the weapon for its discipline.

Ivo had closed the screen. He had breathed in for four counts and out for six, which was a technique Jonah had taught him for suppressant spikes and which worked, annoyingly, on other kinds of poison too.

Beside him, Priya Raman watched the river rewrite itself under the skids. She had not asked. That was why she was the person he could sit next to while his hands did their best impression of calm.

"If you say his name on the ground," Ivo said, "I will reassign your vowels."

Priya did not look away from the glass. "That isn't how linguistics works."

"It is today."

"You could request a different detail."

"I could also request that the canopy relocate." The helicopter shuddered through a thermal. Ivo set his palm on the case between his boots — stylus blanks, sample vials, the tea tin he pretended was not a comfort object. "The Reach will not wait for my personal history to become convenient. Helix will not wait. If I flinch, they will write the flinch into the risk assessment and the next commander will be worse, because he will have been chosen to manage me."

"And this one wasn't."

"This one was chosen because he is good at not dying." Ivo heard how dry he sounded and was grateful for it. Dryness was a clean instrument. "He will not know me. I intend to keep the data set pure."

Priya was quiet for the length of a bend in the river. "You told me once. You were very drunk and very exact. I have not treated it as an anecdote."

"I know."

"If he hurts you again, I will not be linguistic about it."

Ivo almost smiled. It did not complete. "He won't. He doesn't have the context. That's the mercy and the insult."

The insult was the part he could not put in a vial. He had spent years becoming a person who could not be shrunk by a hallway. He had published, and crossed two continents of anomalous ground, and learned how to stand in a room where men with guns waited to see if the omega would ask permission to speak. He did not ask. The work had carved him down to competence, and competence had a scent even blockers couldn't fully hide: rain, antiseptic, the absence of a plea.

And still, at the bottom of the jar, a boy who had not cried.

He did not open that memory on purpose. It opened itself, the way a badly sealed sample did, on the smell of wet paper.

The science fair had been in the east gym, which always smelled of varnish and oranges because the janitor believed in oranges. Ivo's board had taken him eleven months: moth wings pinned in a grid, each vein drawn again by hand, and in the corner a rubbing he had made from a stone fragment in the Harrowgate museum, an inscription no one at St. Briony was meant to care about. He had cared about it with the helpless greed of a person who had finally found a door that opened inward.

Calder Rhys had not come to the fair to see the door. He had come because Ivo had corrected him in history third period — a single sentence, the capital of a dead polity, delivered because the teacher had asked the room and the room had looked at its desks. People had laughed. Not cruelly. That was worse. They had laughed as if Calder were a sport they all shared, and Ivo had made the sport stumble.

After the fair, in the corridor where the fire doors stuck, Calder and two friends had been waiting with a cup of something that might have been soda and was definitely enough. The notebook came off the board. The wings dissolved into gray lace. The inscription blurred into a bruise on the paper. Calder had laughed then, too, a bright, careless sound, the sound of a boy who would be forgiven by tomorrow because he was tall and sorry in advance to everyone except the person on the floor.

"Stay where you belong," he had said, not even angry anymore. Bored, which was a kind of violence. "Ghost."

There had been a shove on a stair two weeks later, and three afternoons in the library before that when Calder had almost been a person, and a day when Calder had told a worse boy to walk on. Ivo kept those in the same drawer. He did not let the almosts erase the cup. He did not let the cup erase the almosts. He had become an adult who could hold both, which was not the same thing as healing, and he refused to call it healing where anyone could quote him.

The helicopter dropped. His stomach stayed briefly behind, a scientist to the last.

"Priya," he said.

"I won't say it."

"Thank you."

Base Lantern appeared as a wound of light in the green. Wire, mast, pad. Figures in rain. Ivo catalogued them because cataloguing was how he landed in his body: a large man at the edge of the pad; a smaller one with a rifle held as if it were a fact; a woman with a medic's bag; a woman at the radio mast who looked up too fast; and the officer in the center, hood down, scar visible even through the rotor-wash, standing as if the storm had been informed of his rank and had agreed to negotiate.

Calder Rhys lifted a hand, two fingers, not a wave so much as a claim on the ground they were about to touch.

Ivo's pulse did a stupid thing. He allowed it to happen and then he filed it under autonomic response, which was accurate and insufficient. The man was beautiful in the way storms were beautiful when you were not yet in them. Confidence sat on him like a well-fitted plate. None of it was the boy, except the eyes, except the way he tipped his head to listen to the woman at his shoulder, except the pause before he stepped forward — a pause Ivo knew from classrooms, the half-second in which Calder decided whether to be decent.

He decided, evidently, to be charming. Ivo watched it happen and felt something old and precise settle behind his ribs. Not longing. Recognition. A specimen does not get to be surprised by its own label.

The skids kissed the pad. The crew chief threw the door. Rain came in with the smell of hot metal and leaves.

Ivo took his case and stepped down into the wash. He did not slip. He had promised himself that, childishly, and he kept the promise. Priya came after him. The rest of the science team would follow on the second bird; he had wanted the first look without a committee. He had not wanted this look. He had taken it anyway.

Calder met him at the edge of the pad, out of the worst of the rotor, and smiled like a man who had never been disliked by anyone who mattered.

"Dr. Maren?" The voice was lower than memory and worse for it. "Major Calder Rhys. I'll be the reason you stay alive long enough to annoy me."

There it was. No flicker. No double take. The surname passed over him like weather he had not been hired to notice. Ivo had prepared for cruelty and for awkwardness and, in a locked drawer, for a recognition that would have required him to be brave in a different key. He had not fully prepared for absence. Absence was cleaner. Absence was also a small, stupid cut.

He shifted the case to his other hand and met the smile with a face he had built for ministries.

"Dr. Maren," he said. "You can call me the asset if it's faster."

Calder's grin sharpened, pleased, as if they had already agreed to a game. "I'll call you alive if you follow the brief."

"We'll see which of those is scientifically achievable."

Behind Calder, the woman with the command posture — captain's tabs, beta, if Ivo's nose was reading the blocker-flat air correctly — made a sound that might have been a laugh she had decided not to spend. Calder didn't look back. His attention was a physical thing. It landed on Ivo's scar, his case, the set of his shoulders, and found nothing it could name.

"Welcome to Lantern," Calder said. "Try not to fall in love with the scenery. It doesn't love you back."

Ivo looked past him at the canopy, at the dark that began where the floodlights ended, at the place he had crossed an ocean to question.

"Most scenery doesn't," he said. "That's why the questions are interesting."

He walked toward the tents. He did not let his hands shake. Priya matched his pace and did not speak a name. The rain was loud enough to cover a heartbeat, and Ivo Maren, who had recognized the man in the first second and would go on recognizing him in every one after, said nothing at all.
