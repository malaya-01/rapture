Dusk in the Orrocanopy was not a color. It was a decision the light made to leave, and it made it fast.

Calder had them on a stone shelf above the water because the shelf had a wall at their backs and a view of the channel, which was as close to virtue as the day was offering. Lantern's journal was in Hester's satchel. The pole with the tool marks was in Maren's case. The print Sable would not call a hoof was in everyone's posture. They were eating, which Mateo had turned into a lawful order, and the ghost had spoken twice without changing its sentence.

Jun saw them first. She did not shout. She said, "Up," and the word was small and finished, and every weapon in the detail found a direction.

They came out of the canopy like pieces of the canopy that had learned to fall on purpose. Translucent membranes, a leading edge that caught the last light and turned it into a knife-line, bodies barely there until they banked and the bank showed you the ribs. Three. Then a fourth, higher, riding the others' wake. Glass kites. Calder had a paragraph on them in the threat annex and a sudden, complete dislike of paragraphs.

"Into the wall," he said. "Harun, noise if they dive. Jun, you have the high one if it commits. Nobody runs for the boats. They like a back."

People moved. Nia took the science cluster and put stone between them and the open air. Ellis stumbled on the lip and went down on an ankle with a sound he tried to swallow. Anouk hauled him. Hester swore with scholarly thoroughness. Maren did not go to the wall.

He had turned to face the open, notebook forgotten, head tilted, watching the leading edge of the nearest kite as if it were a sentence he could finish.

"Maren," Calder said.

"Wait," Maren said. Quiet. The quiet of a man in a laboratory, which was insane, which was also so focused Calder felt the focus hit him like weather. "Look at its movement. It isn't hunting sight. The head is wrong for sight. It's correcting on the dive path after the body has already chosen — scent, or heat. Watch the edge. It twitches when we shift. When the rain—"

"I don't care what it twitches for if it opens you."

The first kite folded and fell.

Calder crossed the shelf in three strides, got a fist in the back of Maren's jacket, and dragged him into the wall so hard they both hit stone. The membrane screamed over the place Maren had been standing. A clean sound, like cloth torn next to the ear. Something hot and thin kissed Jun's sleeve on the flank and she made one clipped noise and did not drop the rifle.

"Rain," Maren said, into the rock, breathless and not afraid enough. "It's starting. They can't hold the line in heavy rain. And they commit along the scent plume. Your blockers — the edges fail when you're sweating. The dive follows the failed edge. Tell her to shoot the leading rib on the high one, not the body. The body isn't where the choice is."

Calder wanted to shake him. He wanted, with a clarity that embarrassed him, to keep his fist in the jacket until the sky was empty. He did the other thing. He listened.

"Jun. High bird. Leading rib, the bright edge, not the center. On my mark the rest of you go still. Don't give it a new plume."

The rain arrived as if it had been queued. The high kite banked, uncertain, the leading edge twitching exactly where Maren had said a choice lived. Jun fired once. The kite snapped sideways, membrane blooming, and went into the channel in a shatter of pale ribs. The other three sheared off into the canopy, disgusted, or finished, or both. Harun put a burst into the trees anyway because Harun believed in punctuation. Calder let him have three rounds and then cut it.

Silence, except the rain, except Ellis saying he was fine in the tone of a man who was not, except the small tear of Jun checking her own arm.

"Grazed," she said. "It's rude. It's not structural."

Calder's hand was still on Maren's jacket. He let go. Maren turned. There was stone-dust on his cheek and a calm on his face that was cracked at the edges, finally, by adrenaline he refused to spend out loud. Up close the pause was there again before he spoke. Calder knew the pause. He knew it the way you knew a room you had stood in with the lights off: the distance to the wall, the smell of pencil shavings, a window. No face. The not-knowing made him angrier than the kite.

"You were told to get to the wall," he said.

"If I had gotten to the wall," Maren said, "you would have shot the body. The body is a kite. The choice is the edge. Jun would have wasted the round, and the high one would have come through on the scent you were all making by running."

"You don't get to be right and dead. Those are different outcomes. I am hired for one of them."

"You used what I saw."

"I used it after I moved you, because I am not required to let you stand in the open to be brilliant." Calder heard his voice and did not sand it. Nia was listening. Let her. "You are not reckless because you are stupid. I have updated my model. You are reckless because the moment something impossible happens, you stop being a person and start being a witness. I need the witness. I need him on the correct side of the stone. Next time you say wait from cover, or I will put you in cover and you can be offended about it in a report."

Maren looked at him. The look was not the boy Calder couldn't find. It was a man taking a measurement and disliking the result because the result was fair.

"All right," Maren said, after the pause. "From cover."

"Jun."

"I heard him," Jun said. She had her sleeve cut already, Mateo's hands incoming. "He was right about the rib. Don't let it become a personality, Doctor. I still had to make the shot."

"I wouldn't take the shot from you," Maren said. "I don't like heights that shoot back."

Jun's mouth did the small treacherous smile. Calder felt something in his chest unclench and then re-clench around a different problem. Respect. It had arrived dressed as fury, which was how he trusted it. The scientist was not fragile. The scientist was not his. The scientist had just kept a corporal's round from being a eulogy, and had nearly become the reason for a different eulogy, and was standing in the rain with dust on his face looking slightly to the left of Calder's eyes, the way the photograph had looked slightly to the left of the lens.

Pencil shavings. A window. A beat before speech.

"Mateo has you after Jun," Calder said, rough. "Ankle and ego, whichever is louder. Ellis first if the ankle is the ego. Nobody wanders off to admire the dead kite. Rhee, if the ghost changes its sentence because we made noise, I want it before you have a theory."

"It just repeated," Rhee said. "Same words. Like it didn't care that we lived."

"Good," Calder said. "Let's disappoint it by continuing."

He turned back to the channel so he would not keep looking at Maren's mouth. The rain came down in earnest, and the canopy took the kites back, and Calder Rhys, who had dragged a man out of the air and then used the man's eyes to kill the thing correctly, stood with his hands empty and could not remember where he had learned the length of that pause — only that he had learned it, once, in a room that had not forgiven him for leaving.
