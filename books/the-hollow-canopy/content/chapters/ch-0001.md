The rain at Forward Base Lantern did not fall so much as occupy the air. Calder Rhys stood on the old survey mast with his hood down and let it find the scar along his jaw, because the alternative was the operations tent, and the operations tent already believed it had won the argument about his mood.

Below him the camp was a geometry of discipline imposed on mud. Two floodlight towers. A ring of wire that would stop a desperate person and inconvenience a jaguar. The helicopter pad, shining like a wet coin. Beyond the wire the Orrocanopy rose in a single black thought, so dense the dawn could not decide whether to bother.

He liked it. That was the sort of thing he was not supposed to say to civilians.

He had been up since the generator changed its note, a little after four, when the night shift's coffee went bitter and the river started showing a skin of mist. The command cot smelled of mildew and the citrus wipe Mateo forced on anything that touched skin. Calder had read the ORCHID packet under a lamp hooded with a spare shirt. Light was a courtesy. You did not spend it on the tree line for the sake of your own eyes.

Dr. I. Maren. The initial annoyed him on principle. Institutes hid people inside initials until the person became a problem with a bibliography. Omega. Heavy field suppressant, logged, countersigned, the sort of paperwork that meant someone in a dry office had imagined this jungle and then flinched. A line in the Directorate annex, typed and then underlined by a hand that was not his: *Protect the knowledge. Recover the asset. Do not recover the site if the site begins to answer.*

He had read that twice. People wrote orders like that after they had already lost someone and were trying to make the loss sound like a policy.

The photograph he had saved for last, which was a mistake, because last things stuck. A man looking slightly left of the lens. Dark hair. A thin scar through one eyebrow. A mouth at rest the way other people rested their hands, deliberately. Calder had felt a snag behind his sternum, small and insulting. He did not forget faces. He had built a career on not forgetting faces. The snag had no name attached, so he had called it prejudice and laced his boots.

Prejudice was faster than mystery. Mystery made soldiers inventive, and inventive soldiers got civilians killed.

He had come up the mast to spend eight minutes being no one's decision. The eight minutes were over. The river below the pad moved like a muscle. A Virelian sentry at the wire coughed into his collar and pretended he had not been watching the major stand in the rain like a man auditioning for a statue. Calder lifted two fingers. The sentry lifted two back, relieved to be returned to the ordinary religion of looking outward.

His earpiece clicked. Nia, who had opinions about men standing in weather as if weather were a personality test.

"If you are composing a tragic speech," she said, "the asset lands in twenty-two minutes and the speech will not fit in the briefing."

"I was composing breakfast."

"You ate."

"I was composing yours. You get cruel when you skip it."

"I get accurate." A pause, filled with the soft hash of rain on her mic. "Blocker."

Calder touched the patch behind his ear. Still seated. Still doing the unromantic work of keeping an alpha from smelling like a threat in a room full of people who had not agreed to be threatened. "Current. You may stand down from mothering me."

"I will stand down when the scientist is on the boat and you have not called him a liability in his hearing."

"I would never."

"You would, and then you would be charming about it, and then I would have to file the charm as an incident." She sighed. "Come down, Major. Rhee has a ghost."

That got him off the mast.

The operations tent smelled of wet canvas, coffee, and the particular ozone of radios that had been asked to do too much. Harun Dalca had claimed the end of the table by the simple method of being larger than the map. Jun Park sat on an ammo crate with her rifle across her knees, oiling a part that did not need oiling, which meant she was listening to everything. Mateo Solano had a med bag open and was counting suppressant vials with the expression of a man who expected the count to personally disappoint him. Rhee Amari did not look up from the set. Her headphones were around her neck. The speaker between her hands hissed, found a syllable, and lost it again.

Calder ducked in, shook rain off like a dog, and grinned because it made Harun grin back. Leadership, he had learned, was often just the decision to be the least frightened person in a room that had earned its fear.

"Report," he said.

"Camp is boring," Harun said. "I object."

"Noted and discarded." Calder looked at Rhee. "Ghost."

She rolled the dial a fraction. The hiss organized itself. A man's voice, distant, as if it had been walking toward the microphone for years and had not arrived.

"—don't ask it—"

Static. Nineteen seconds of rain and a generator. Then the same phrase, same wear on the same vowel, as if someone had put a single breath in a jar.

"Old survey band," Rhee said. "Not our net. Not encrypted, which is either arrogant or dead. It repeats every nineteen minutes. Bearing is useless — the canopy folds the signal — but the strength climbs when I aim the mast inland. I logged four cycles before I called you. I wanted to be sure I wasn't naming weather."

"You sat on it," Nia said, from the tent flap. She had a way of arriving as a correction.

Rhee's mouth tightened. "I sat on it for an hour. Then I was sure."

Calder held up a hand before Nia could spend the hour. "Source?"

"Could be a stuck beacon from Survey Lantern. Could be someone alive with a looped recording and a bad sense of theater. Could be the Reach doing what the Reach does when it wants attention." Rhee met his eyes. She was young and disliked looking young, which made her brave in the specific way of people who had already apologized to themselves for being right. "I don't like the cadence. It isn't a distress call. It's an instruction."

"Don't ask it," Jun said, almost idly. "Ask it what?"

"Excellent question," Calder said. "We will not shout it into the trees until the person we are paid to keep alive is done explaining why the trees might answer." He tapped the map. Puerto Virel on the coast. The river like a vein. A red pencil circle where the last confident cartography gave up and became rumor. "Sable is still out?"

"Two-kilometer loop," Nia said. "She'll be back before the bird. She found boot-adjacent prints and is offended that I called them boot-adjacent."

"Tell her she can be offended on the boat." Calder pulled the packet from the dry bag. The seal had already been broken by his own hands at 0400; he broke it again now so the team could see him do it. Theater mattered. Secrets that looked like secrets made soldiers invent worse ones.

"Asset designation ORCHID," he said. "Directorate of Anomalous Ecology. Dr. I. Maren. Omega. Suppressants verified twelve hours before takeoff, and Mateo will verify them again before we push upriver, because I enjoy living and because Jonah Abebe will skin me if the log is cute instead of true. The doctor is the leading field authority on anomalous ecosystems and whatever else they don't want printed on a slide. Several governments would like him. One private company would like him more. Helix Meridian had a contract until they decided ethics were a suggestion. If Helix is in this rain, they are not here for the orchids."

Harun cracked his knuckles, which was not a plan. "So we shoot the company and carry the doctor."

"We avoid the company, we do not start a war the Compact has not authorized, and we carry the doctor only if he has done something impressively stupid." Calder flipped to the photograph.

The man in it was looking slightly to the left of the lens, as if the interesting part of the room had declined to be captured. Dark hair. A thin scar through one eyebrow. A lab coat worn like a concession. The mouth was at rest in a way that suggested rest was a skill. Calder felt a small, irritant snag behind his sternum — the feeling of a word on the tip of the tongue, insulting because he was not a man who forgot words.

He decided the snag was prejudice and kept going. Prejudice was faster.

"He will be arrogant," Calder said. "Field academics usually are. They have been the smartest person in safe rooms for so long that they think a jaguar will be impressed by a citation. He will be fragile in the ways that don't show up on a PT test. He will wander. Our job is to be the wall between him and his curiosity, and also between him and anyone who wants what he knows. Nia has command if I am occupied with the wall. Mateo has medical authority over designation events — if I tell you to leave a tent, you leave the tent. That includes me."

Jun's cloth paused. "You expecting a heat?"

"I'm expecting a jungle that does not care what we expect." He said it lightly. He meant it as law. "No one uses scent to make a point. No one jokes about the asset's designation where he can hear, or where he can't. We are not a barracks story. We are a detail. Questions?"

Mateo lifted a vial. "His dose is the heavy field compound. It'll keep a heat from breaking unless something actively strips it. If something strips it, I want betas on the watch and alphas outside the cordon. I'll say that again when he's here, to his face, so he knows it isn't about him being difficult."

"Good," Calder said. "Rhee, keep the ghost on a loop recorder. Don't answer it. Don't ask it anything, since it went to the trouble of being specific. Harun, you and Jun have the pad. If the pilot is early I want him waved off the wire, not onto it. Nia—"

"I already hate your speech," she said. "Give it to me anyway."

He smiled at her, the real one, the one that did not perform. "If I start managing him like a problem and forget he's a person, you kick me. You've earned the right."

"I earned it by watching you be good at this," she said. "Don't make me spend it."

Outside, the rain changed pitch. Rotors, still far, beating the air into a shape.

Calder looked at the photograph again. Dr. I. Maren, who had not bothered to face the camera, who would land in a few minutes and require protecting from the forest, from Helix, and from himself.

The snag behind Calder's sternum did not leave. He closed the packet.

He closed the packet. The snag stayed where he had put it, which was to say it did not stay put at all.

They used the twelve minutes the way a detail used any twelve minutes it might not get again. Harun walked the pad's cable line and kicked a coil that had been trying to become a trap. Jun climbed the sandbag stack at the east corner, settled, and became a fact the rain could not move. Mateo staged a kit where the rotor wash would not eat it: trauma, antiemetic, the confirmation vials for a suppressant draw he would not do on a landing pad unless someone was already dying. Rhee dragged a spare headset to the mast and muttered frequencies like a woman saying grace in a language she had invented to keep from swearing.

Nia fell in beside Calder on the duckboard between the tent and the wire.

"You're doing the face," she said.

"I have several. Be specific."

"The one where you've already disliked a man for the crime of needing you." She watched the inland dark, not him. That was how she told the truth without making it a ceremony. "Three years ago I let an asset talk me into a corridor because she was brilliant and I wanted the brilliance to mean she was also careful. She wasn't. The corridor was. I have spent every briefing since being ruder than I am, so that I do not get to bury someone I liked for being right."

Calder was quiet. The rotors were still a rumor with a pulse. "You want me rude."

"I want you accurate. If he's fragile, carry him. If he's arrogant, fence him. If he's both, do not pick the version that lets you make a joke and call it leadership. And if that photograph is sitting in your head for a reason you can't name, you tell me before the river. Not after a body."

"It's a photograph of a man who wouldn't look at a camera. That's a personality, not a history."

"Histories love a personality." She bumped his shoulder with hers, brief, the way you checked a strap. "Go be tall at them. I'll be the one counting."

Sable's voice came through the net, breathy with pace, not with fear. "Loop's clean. I'm four minutes from the wire. Boot-adjacent prints on the north cut, old, and a drag mark I refuse to call a drag mark until I've sworn at it. Do not let the bird land on my head. I just washed this rifle in a spiritual sense."

"Copy," Calder said. "Come in on the west gap. Jun has the pad. If you're late, you get the second bird's mud."

"Cruelty noted."

He stepped off the duckboard into the pad's open light. Rain needled the scar. He rolled his shoulders once, not because they hurt, because the body liked a signal that the waiting was over and the work had a shape. Behind him the camp did what camps did when aircraft were incoming: voices dropped, weapons stopped being toys, the generator seemed louder because people had stopped competing with it.

Harun grinned at him across the cable. "You going to smile at the doctor or save it for the widows?"

"I'm going to smile at everyone and then do what Nia tells me. It's a system."

"It's a religion," Jun said from the bags, without looking down. "Don't convert the asset on the pad. They get slippery when they're inspired."

Calder laughed, once, the real size of it. The sound didn't travel. The canopy took small sounds and spent them on itself.

The helicopter arrived as a change in the rain's grain, then as a light, then as a wind that shoved the puddles sideways and turned the pad into a bright, stupid target. Calder raised his hand, two fingers, not a wave so much as a claim on the ground they were about to share. He could see the cabin glass and, behind it, a shape that might have been a man holding a case as if the case were the only solid argument in the air.

"Positions," he said, though they were already in them. "Let's go meet the man who thinks he's the mission."

The skids came down through the wash. The jungle — vast, wet, and uninterested in rank — waited at the wire like a breath it had been holding since before any of them had ranks at all.
