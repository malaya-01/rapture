On the sixth day the river gave up pretending it was the way in.

The channel split around a comb of rock, and the right-hand split was a staircase that had lost an argument with a fig. Ivo saw the geometry before he let himself believe it: treads too regular for water, a rise that repeated, a lip carved to shed rain and then buried by the rain's allies. Roots thick as bodies had taken the stair and made it a suggestion. Under the suggestion, stone. Pale where the moss tore. Fitted. Intentional.

He stood in the boat with his hand on the case and did not speak until the count in his head matched the count in his eyes.

"There," he said.

Calder, at the motor, followed his look. The charm he wore in safe minutes was not on. He had been thinner with it since the kites, as if respect had cost him a habit. "Nia. Cut. Maren has a stair."

They landed on a shelf of gravel that had once been a landing and now grew a crop of black orchids, small, waxy, smelling faintly of metal. Ivo disliked the smell and recorded it. Metal without metal was a sentence. He was collecting sentences. He had not yet decided which ones were allowed to be questions.

The causeway began above the stair. It ran inland under the canopy, a spine of fitted blocks wide enough for four people, broken where roots had shouldered through, continuing anyway with the arrogance of a state that had not asked the forest's permission. On the third intact span, a roof comb rose and vanished into leaves — not a whole temple, a tooth of one, carved with knots that made Priya make a sound Ivo had only heard from her in archives.

"Don't touch them yet," she said, to everyone, including herself. "The shadow's wrong. This script doesn't like being read at noon. It likes an angle. If I force it I'll invent a god Anouk has to kill."

"Please invent a god," Anouk said. "Hester needs the exercise."

Hester was not listening. She had her palm flat on a block, eyes closed, as if the stone might still be Pavel's colleague. When she opened them she looked older and more awake. "This is not a camp," she said. "This is a road with a memory of a city. He was looking for a road. The file gave him a river instead."

No one answered. The ghost on Rhee's set chose that minute to speak, and under the leaves the voice sounded nearer, as if the stone were a better conductor than the water.

*Don't ask it.*

Ivo felt the hum then. Not a sound. A pressure in the teeth, the same nerve that hated the dentist and certain laboratories, a low continuity under the rain. The Vein, if the old reports were allowed to use their own vocabulary. He did not name it aloud. Naming it would be a kind of asking. He set his tongue against his teeth and endured the continuity and watched Calder's back as the major went first onto the span, because of course he went first, because walls, if they were any good, arrived before the thing they were walling.

The back was broader than the boy's. The walk was a soldier's. None of that helped. The city — and it was a city, Ivo knew it the way you knew a language from three words — felt like the project they had failed.

Three afternoons in the St. Briony library. A table by the window. Rain. A chapter on vanished cities that Calder had actually read and then pretended, the next day, not to have understood, because his friends were in the hall and understanding was a kind of nakedness. Ivo had watched him choose the hall. He had not begged. He had gone back to the moths, and later the moths had been poured out, and the inscription with them.

He could say the name now. It sat in his mouth, ridiculous, enormous, two syllables that would crack the expedition into a before and an after. Calder would turn. The face he would make would be the face from the corridor or the face from the library or some third face Ivo had no protocol for. Priya would hear. Priya would not forgive him for spending it on a causeway when he had made her promise.

He swallowed the name.

"The joints are mortared with something that isn't lime," he said, instead. His voice came out level. He was proud of that and tired of being proud of it. "There's grit in it. The same family as the wing dust. They built with the dust, or the dust colonized the build. I need a sample from a break that's already broken. I won't make a new one."

Calder glanced back. The glance caught on Ivo and snagged, the way it had been snagging since the pad: a man patting his pockets for a key he was sure he owned. "You can have a break that's already broken. You can't have the next span until Sable says it isn't a mouth. Agreed, or do we get to relitigate the tape?"

"Agreed."

"Look at you. A schedule I can live in."

"Don't be charming about it. The stone can hear you."

It was almost a joke. Calder took it as one, and the smile that answered was smaller than his public smiles, meant for a shorter distance. Ivo looked at the mortared joint until the smile had somewhere else to go. His hands were steady. He checked, because he was his own unreliable instrument. Steady. Good. The tremor was for afterward, and afterward had not been scheduled.

They went in under the comb.

The light changed. Green became a darker green, and then a color that was only stone's idea of green. Carvings along the inner wall showed processions of people carrying not weapons but vessels, and among the vessels shapes that were not people and not quite animals — kneeling forms, ribbons, a long body in water. Ivo's mind reached for categories and he made it wait. Categories were how you missed the system. The pressure in his teeth increased as the causeway dipped, and the rain, shut out by leaves and stone, became a memory the rock was keeping for them.

Behind him, Nia counted softly. Ahead, Calder set his foot on each block as if the block had to be convinced. Priya's breath caught on a knot and released when she refused to translate it yet. Ellis whispered to a moth that had followed them in, telling it, absurdly, that it was doing very well. Sable told him to whisper less. Harun ducked a root and called the root a coward. Jun did not waste a word. Jonah watched pulses with his eyes. Anouk and Hester entered the same silence from opposite doors. Tomas Ibarra was days behind them, holding a dock, already part of a different weather.

Ivo Maren walked into Qalen-Sar with the name unsaid and the Vein in his teeth and the man who had ruined his notebook leading him, unknowing, into the city they had once tried to imagine from a library book and had not been old enough, or kind enough, to deserve.

The causeway turned. The first chamber opened, black water at its lip, stone steps going down into a dark that hummed.

No one asked it anything.

They went in anyway.
